BRUTE FORCE

A hands-on look at how attackers guess passwords by trying millions of combinations — and why the passwords you pick decide whether that works.

01

What is a brute force attack?

A brute force attack is a simple idea: a computer guesses a password over and over, trying every possible combination of letters, numbers, and symbols until one of them works.

There's no cleverness involved. The computer doesn't "figure out" your password — it just tries "a", then "b", then "c"... all the way through every possible combination, at incredible speed.

This works because computers are fast and patient. A person guessing passwords by hand might try a few per minute. A computer can try thousands, millions, or even billions per second, depending on how the password is stored and how it's being attacked.

03

Why length beats cleverness

Every extra character you add to a password multiplies the number of guesses an attacker has to try. That's because the possible combinations grow exponentially, not just a little bit at a time.

A 4-character password made only of lowercase letters has about 450,000 possible combinations. Add uppercase letters, numbers, and symbols, and stretch it to 12 characters, and that number grows past a trillion trillion.

"Online login form" means guessing through the actual sign-in page, which is usually slowed down on purpose. "Stolen password file" means an attacker already has a copy of scrambled passwords and is guessing offline on fast hardware — which is why protecting that data in the first place matters so much.

04

How to defend against it

  • Use long passphrases. Four random words like "correct horse battery staple" are easier to remember and harder to crack than a short, complex-looking password.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA). Even if an attacker guesses your password, they still need your phone or security key to get in.
  • Never reuse passwords. If one site leaks your password, attackers will try it everywhere else you might have an account.
  • Use a password manager. It generates and remembers long, random passwords for you, so you don't have to.
  • Rate limiting and lockouts. This is on the defender's side: websites that slow down or lock out repeated failed logins make brute force attacks far less practical.
